Research · after the first round · November 2026

We tested our own model. Here is what held up.

After the PJDSC 2026 first round we reviewed the published work, ran three other methods through the same test, and traced every loss to its cause. The submitted model is unchanged; this page reports what we learned about it.

1 · Literature review

Built on published work, filling a Philippine gap.

We compared 19 studies. No Philippine forecasting study among them has all four of these; EpiLag-PH does.

Many areas, every week

104 areas, 2 to 8 weeks ahead. Earlier Philippine forecasts cover the national total, one metro area or one city.

Calibrated ranges

A likely range for every forecast, with the share of real counts inside it reported for each test year.

Alerts scored both ways

Outbreak starts caught and the share of alerts that were false, not only the hits.

Several unseen years

Including a record epidemic (2019), a reporting collapse (2020) and a flat year (2023).

What the literature says drives dengue

Rain, 5–7 weeks before

Risk peaks at moderate amounts, not the heaviest. Sumi 2017, Carvajal 2018, Iguchi 2018

Humidity, about 4 weeks before

The top weather factor in Metro Manila, and linked to the second seasonal peak. Carvajal 2018, Xu 2020

Temperature, peaking near 27.5 °C

Risk rises, peaks, then falls, across 61 Philippine provinces. Seposo 2023, Mordecai 2017

What we do not claim. Errors cannot be compared across studies, because places, scales and case counts differ. So we do not call EpiLag-PH the most accurate dengue model, and we did not test it against an LSTM.
2 · Four methods, one test

No method wins everywhere. LightGBM wins most.

Negative binomial regression, a quantile random forest and SARIMAX took the same test and the same calibration as the submitted LightGBM model: each test year forecast only from earlier years.

Negative binomial won the hard years

Flat 2023 at every lead time, and the 2019 epidemic for regions at 2 and 4 weeks. It carries a rise forward where trees cannot.

SARIMAX won further ahead

Regions in 2019 at 6–8 weeks and in 2025 at 4–8 weeks. It caught the most outbreak starts on Watch (1,102 of 1,260) but the fewest on Outbreak warning (17%).

Random forest never won

It stayed close to LightGBM, at a median of 2.6% higher error.

Why not average the four? An equal average had the lowest error in 11 of 24 checks and beat "same as this week" in all 24, against 19 for LightGBM alone. But it caught fewer outbreak starts on Outbreak warning: 30% against 36%. For an early-warning tool, missing outbreaks costs more than a small gain in error. The idea of averaging was also picked after seeing these test years.
3 · What drives the forecast

Recent cases lead. Weather matters further ahead.

Recent cases carry 48–76% of the model's attention. For regions and Quezon City, weather's share grows with every extra week of lead time, which matches the mosquito-cycle lags in the literature.

4 · Where it lost, and why

Three losses, three causes.

We report the years the submitted model lost, and checked each explanation against the data before accepting it.

18% worse

2019, two weeks ahead

The record epidemic rose beyond anything in the training years, and tree models cannot forecast past their training range. Negative binomial carried the rise forward instead.

4% worse

2023, the flat year

The flattest year in the data: its peak was only 1.94 times its average week. In such a year, "same as this week" is a strong forecast.

92% not 80%

2023, a range too wide

The calibration model ran about 18% low on 2022. Our single margin (0.227, against 0.094 and 0.097 in the next two years) then widened both ends of the 2023 range, though only the top had missed.

Before calibration the range is too narrow in every test year. Calibration fixes that in five of six; in 2023 it overshoots. Ruled out as causes: an unusual 2022, and the missing 2020–21 data. Details: research/analysis/causes.md.

5 · What the research found

A better set of variables, not yet proven.

Adding neighbours' cases for provinces, and heat and humidity for regions and Quezon City, beat the submitted model in 21 of 24 checks, at a median of −1.4% error.

No outbreak catches lost

Watch caught 1,050 outbreak starts against 1,049, of 1,260. Outbreak warning caught 473 against 458.

Two more wins over the simple rule

It beat "same as this week" in 21 of 24 checks, against 19. The two new ones are Regions + QC 2023 at 2 and 4 weeks, the year the submitted model lost.

Why it is not the submitted model. These variables were chosen after seeing the test years, and no unseen year is left to score them on. The gain is optimistic until a new season is scored, so the app, the open data and Dengue Drill still run the submitted model.
6 · Limits and next steps

What this does not show, and what comes next.

Each limit below is one we can name a fix for.

Limits

  • Where the data end. Province data stop in January 2021, so provinces have two test years, both unusual. Regional data skip 2020–2021.
  • Reports, not infections. The model forecasts reported cases, which undercount infections.
  • Watch raises many false alarms (42–63%). Outbreak warning is stricter but catches fewer outbreak starts.
  • Calibration rests on one year, with one margin for both ends of the range.

Next steps

  1. Score the new variables on a season they have not seen.
  2. Calibrate on more than one year, or set the lower and upper margins separately.
  3. Test an average of the methods that keeps Outbreak-warning catches.
  4. Add weekly province data after 2021.
