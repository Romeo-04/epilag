EpiLag-PH: what the research found

EpiLag-PH forecasts weekly dengue cases 2–8 weeks ahead, with a calibrated 10–90% range and two outbreak alerts, for 86 provinces, 17 regions and Quezon City. After the first round we reviewed the published work, tested the model against three other methods and nine new groups of variables, and traced each result to its cause.

Key findings

  1. EpiLag-PH fills a gap in the Philippine work. We reviewed 19 studies, 9 of them Philippine. None of the Philippine forecasting studies combines weekly forecasts for many areas, calibrated ranges, alerts scored for both catches and false alarms, and testing on several unseen years. EpiLag-PH does all four.
  2. The submitted model holds up on unseen years. It beat “same as this week” and “same week last year” in 5 of 6 test years. The exception is Regions + QC 2023, the flattest year in the data, where it was 4% worse than “same as this week”.
  3. No method wins everywhere; LightGBM wins most often. On the same test and the same calibration, LightGBM had the lowest error in 12 of 24 checks, negative binomial in 7, SARIMAX in 5 and random forest in none.
  4. Weather helps the regional model more the further ahead it looks. For Regions + QC, adding temperature (the heat group) to recent cases and season cut the error by 2% at 2 weeks and 7% at 8 weeks, in line with the 4–18 week mosquito and virus lags in the literature. Provinces showed no measurable gain.
  5. Each loss has a specific cause. 2019 was a record epidemic, beyond anything in the training years. 2023 was flat, so “same as this week” was hard to beat. 2023’s range was too wide (92% against 80%) because the calibration model forecast 2022 too low and the single margin widened both ends. The 2020–21 data gap was tested and ruled out.
  6. A better set of variables was found, but it is not yet proven. Neighbours’ cases for provinces, and temperature and humidity for regions, were better in 21 of 24 checks (median −1.4% error). They were chosen after seeing the test years, so the submitted model stays unchanged until a new season scores them.

The gap EpiLag-PH fills

  1. Weekly forecasts for many Philippine areas at lead times a health office can act on.
  2. Ranges calibrated to a stated coverage, with coverage reported per test year.
  3. Alerts scored for both outbreak starts caught and false alarms.
  4. Several unseen test years, including unusual ones, against naive baselines.

What we do not claim

  • “Most accurate dengue model in the Philippines”: errors are not comparable across studies, and there is no shared benchmark.
  • “Better than LSTM”: we did not test one.
  • “Weather drives the forecast”: recent cases carry most of the model’s attention.

2Methods

Data

  • Cases: weekly reported cases for 86 province-level units, 2016 to January 2021 (DOH Epidemiology Bureau, via HDX). 17 regions (2016–2019 and 2022–2025) and Quezon City (2010–2025), from the UPRI-NOAH data set [pelitro2026].
  • Weather: ERA5 daily weather via Open-Meteo.
  • Population: PSA-based.

Model

  • For each lead time (2, 4, 6, 8 weeks), three LightGBM quantile models: the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles.
  • Inputs: recent cases (last 4 weeks, 4- and 12-week averages, trend, same week last year), 12 weeks of rain, week of year and population. Provinces also have temperature and humidity.
  • Why LightGBM: the drivers act non-linearly, boosted trees learn such shapes and train fast [ke2017], and SHAP explains each forecast.

Calibration

  • Conformalized quantile regression [romano2019]. A copy of the model is fit without the last training year; its misses on that year set a margin.
  • The margin is the 80% conformal quantile of the misses, in log space, and is added to both ends of the range.

Alerts

  • Epidemic line for week w: mean + 2 SD of weeks w−2 to w+2 in earlier years only.
  • Outbreak start: the first week of a run of 2 or more weeks above the line.
  • Watch: the 90% forecast crosses the line. Outbreak warning: the middle forecast crosses it.

The test

  • Each test year is forecast by a model trained only on earlier years, for every week of that year.
  • Every method and variable set takes the same test, with the same rows, calibration and alert scoring.
  • The research harness reproduces the submitted model’s published scores exactly (largest difference 0).

What was compared

  • Methods: negative binomial regression, quantile random forest, SARIMAX, and an equal average of all four.
  • Variables: temperature, humidity, ENSO, elevation, density, neighbours’ cases, heatwave days, a rain anomaly, and rain 12–23 weeks back. Each was added to recent cases + season, and removed from the full set.
  • Candidate final sets, scored on error, range and both alerts.

3.4 Variables proposed by the literature review

  • Neighbours’ cases (this week’s incidence in the nearest areas) helped provinces in all 8 checks on top of the full research set, median −2.8%. It is the only proposed group that added to what the model already had.
  • Heatwave days, a rain anomaly (drought) and rain 12–23 weeks back added nothing on top. The model does attend to rain 12–15 weeks back, but that mostly marks the time of year, which the model already knows.

Sources: research/results/proposed_features.csv, rain_lags.csv, rain_lag_shap.csv.

3.5 Final variables

Provinces
The submitted features + neighbours’ cases. Better in 8 of 8 checks (median −2.4%), and 7 more outbreak starts caught on Watch and 17 more on Outbreak warning.
Regions + QC
The submitted features + temperature and humidity, without ENSO. Better in 13 of 16 checks (median −0.9%, worst +2.1%), with Outbreak warning nearly level (99 against 101 starts).
Together
Better than the submitted model in 21 of 24 checks, median −1.4% (−6.1% to +2.1%). Beat “same as this week” in 21 of 24, against 19; the two new wins are Regions + QC 2023 at 2 and 4 weeks. Range held 80%. Watch caught 1,050 outbreak starts against 1,049; Outbreak warning 473 against 458.
Left out
ENSO: its extra gain is mostly in 2023, it costs 10 Outbreak-warning catches, and with about one El Niño cycle in the data it may act as a label for the year. The average of the four methods catches the fewest outbreak starts on Outbreak warning.

Why the submitted model stays unchanged for the final round

Neighbours’ cases, dropping ENSO and the average were all chosen after seeing the test years, so their gains are optimistic. No unseen year is left to confirm them: province data end in January 2021 and regional data in December 2025. The submitted model’s scores come from a clean test, so it remains the main result. The final variables are the first thing to score on the next season.

Sources: research/results/candidates.csv; research/results/README.md, “Final variables”.

4Why the results came out as they did

Each explanation was checked against the data, then weighed against the literature. Details and every check are in research/analysis/causes.md.

QuestionVerdictEvidence
Why did EpiLag-PH lose to “same as this week” in 2023?Supported: a flat year2023 had the lowest peak-to-mean ratio (1.94) and the lowest 4-week volatility (0.40) in the data. In a flat year, “same as this week” is a strong forecast.
Why was it 18% worse than that baseline in Regions + QC 2019, 2 weeks ahead?Supported: a record yearThe 2019 epidemic rose beyond anything in the training years, and trees cannot forecast past their training range. Negative binomial, which is log-linear, carried the rise forward.
Why is the 2020 “same week last year” score so poor?SupportedIt copied the 2019 epidemic into a year 79% lower nationally, and lower in all 86 provinces. The drop starts in late March 2020.
Does weather matter more further ahead?Supported for Regions + QC, not provincesPublished lags are about 4 weeks for humidity, 5–7 for rain and 10–18 for temperature. A 2-week forecast is made after most of that weather has already shown up in cases.
Does weather help most in unusual years?Not supportedIt helped most in 2024 (−9% at 4 weeks), whose rain was within 10% of normal; little in very wet 2025 or dry 2019. Extremes sit outside the training range, and very wet weather lowers risk [ith2025].
Why are raw ranges too narrow?Partly supportedYears differ, and LightGBM’s raw quantiles are also narrower than the other methods’ (46–73% held, against 58–89%).

Why the 2023 range was too wide

The 2023 range held 92% of weeks against an 80% target. Its raw range was normal (68%); the calibration margin was not. We first tested whether the 2020–21 data gap was to blame: “same week last year” is missing for 17 of 18 areas in 2022. Filling it from 2019, or dropping it, left the 2023 margin at 0.216–0.228, against 0.227. The 4- and 12-week averages, also missing in early 2022, are not the cause either (0.224).   • The calibration copy forecast 2022 about 18% too low, so its misses were one-sided: 38% of weeks above its 90% forecast, 9% below its 10% forecast.
  • The margin is one number added to both ends, so a forecast that ran low also widened the bottom of the 2023 range, where nothing was missed.
  • 2019 had the same pattern (0.176), but the record epidemic used up the extra width: its range held 70%.
  • Hypothesis, not tested: the 2018 and 2022 calibration copies were fit on regional data that were short (2016–2017) or two years old (2016–2019).

5Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • Where the data end. Province data stop in January 2021, so provinces have two test years, both unusual (2019 and 2020). Regional data skip 2020–2021.
  • Choices made after seeing the test years: the average of the four, neighbours’ cases and dropping ENSO. Their gains are optimistic.
  • Reports, not infections. The model forecasts reported cases, which undercount infections, about 4.7 times in one Cebu City study [undurraga2017].
  • One weather point per area.
  • Watch has a high false-alarm share (42–63%). Outbreak warning is stricter but catches fewer outbreak starts.
  • Calibration rests on one year, with one margin for both ends (the 2023 case).

Next steps

  1. Score the final variables on a season they have not seen, such as 2026 once its data are published.
  2. Calibrate on more than one year, or set the lower and upper margins separately [romano2019].
  3. Test a calibrated average of the methods that keeps Outbreak-warning catches.
  4. Add province data after 2021 when it becomes available.
